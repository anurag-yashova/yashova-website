Healthcare advertising is the category where good intentions meet strict policy. Most campaigns fail review not because of what they offer, but because of how they describe the person they are targeting.

## The rule that catches everyone

Ad platforms prohibit implying that you know something about the user's personal health condition.

Rejected: *Suffering from back pain? Our clinic can help.*
Accepted: *Advanced physiotherapy care in Faridabad. Book a consultation.*

The first implies knowledge of the individual. The second describes the service. Same business, same audience, one passes.

Rewrite every ad from the second-person accusation into a first-person description of what you do.

## What else to avoid

- Before-and-after imagery, particularly for cosmetic and weight-related services
- Guaranteed outcomes of any kind
- Superlative claims — "best," "number one" — without substantiation
- Fear-based framing about untreated conditions
- Prescription medication promotion

## Creative that works within the rules

**Credentials.** Doctor qualifications, years practising, institutional affiliation. Trust in healthcare is transferred from the practitioner, not the brand.

**Facility and process.** Show the actual clinic, equipment, and what a visit looks like. This addresses the real anxiety, which is uncertainty about the experience.

**Access and convenience.** Timings, location, availability, insurance accepted. Often the actual decision factor.

**Patient education.** Genuinely useful information about a procedure or condition, offered without a diagnosis attached. This performs well and passes review comfortably.

## Cost expectations

Healthcare cost per lead in India typically runs ₹150–₹600 depending on specialty, geography and competition. High-value specialties run considerably higher.

The relevant number is not cost per lead but **cost per patient who actually attends**. Healthcare leads have a large gap between enquiry and appearance, and campaigns optimising on enquiries alone will happily produce enquiries that never arrive.

## The follow-up problem specific to healthcare

Medical enquiries decay unusually fast. Someone researching a procedure at 11pm has a different intent by morning.

Automated confirmation within seconds, with the clinic name, what they enquired about, and available slots, materially improves attendance. Follow-up should offer a specific appointment time rather than asking the patient to choose from an open calendar.

## Privacy discipline

Do not put health information into your tracking payloads. Event names, custom parameters and lead descriptions should never contain condition, diagnosis or treatment details.

This is both a platform policy matter and a basic obligation to the patient. Track that a lead occurred and its source. Do not track what they were worried about.

## Education as an acquisition channel

The strongest healthcare campaigns we have seen work like education, not advertising.

On a medical coding certification account, the entire funnel ran on free webinars with a credentialed instructor — 15,000 leads at ₹126, 780 enrolments. The mechanism was credibility transferred through teaching, and it applies equally to clinics: a doctor explaining a procedure clearly earns more trust than any claim about the facility.

## What to build

1. Service-descriptive ads that never address a personal condition
2. Landing pages leading with practitioner credentials
3. Booking with specific available slots, not an open form
4. Automated confirmation within seconds
5. Reminder sequence before the appointment
6. Tracking that records source and outcome, never condition
