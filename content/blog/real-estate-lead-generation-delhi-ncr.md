Real estate is the category where cost per lead lies most convincingly. You can generate property enquiries in Delhi NCR at ₹30 all day. Almost none of them will visit a site.

The work is not in getting leads. It is in getting leads a sales team can actually convert.

## The volume trap

A broad campaign offering "luxury 3BHK, price on request" will produce enormous lead volume, because the ad asks nothing and promises everything.

Then the calls start. Half the numbers do not connect. A quarter were browsing. A tenth wanted a different budget entirely. The sales team stops calling the list by day three, and everyone concludes that digital does not work for property.

The campaign was never the problem. The absence of qualification was.

## Qualify hard, inside the form

For property, three questions do most of the filtering:

- **Budget range**, as brackets rather than an open field
- **Timeline** — this month, three months, six months, exploring
- **Purpose** — end use or investment

Adding these will cut your lead volume substantially. It will also mean the leads that remain have self-identified as buyers rather than browsers, and your cost per *qualified* lead usually improves even as cost per raw lead rises.

Measure the right one.

## Put the price in the ad

"Price on request" is a legacy of print. Online, it filters nothing and attracts everyone.

Stating a starting price does three things: it disqualifies people who cannot afford it before you pay for their click, it signals confidence, and it improves ad relevance, which lowers cost per click.

If the developer forbids stating price, state the range or the EMI. Something numeric.

## Speed matters more here than almost anywhere

A property enquiry has a short half-life. The buyer is enquiring on four projects in one evening.

Automated WhatsApp confirmation within seconds — project name, location, starting price, a link to the floor plan — puts you in the conversation before the competitor calls tomorrow.

Then structure human follow-up in waves: same day, next day, day four, day seven, day fourteen. Most site visits in this market are booked on the second or third contact, and most teams stop after the first.

## Geography is a real lever

Meta's default location setting includes people merely *interested* in an area, not only those present in it. For a Faridabad or Gurgaon project, that quietly widens spend to irrelevant audiences.

Set targeting to people living in the area. Then layer proximity: buyers for a Faridabad project frequently come from within Faridabad and adjacent Delhi zones, not from across the country.

For NRI-focused inventory, run that as a separate campaign with separate creative and separate expectations — never blended into the domestic campaign.

## Creative that produces site visits

Static price cards underperform. What works in this category:

- Walkthrough video of the actual property, shot on a phone if necessary
- Construction progress updates, which signal legitimacy
- Location advantage framed as commute time, not as a map
- Possession timeline stated plainly

The recurring theme is evidence. Buyers in Delhi NCR have been burned by delayed projects, and every credibility signal you provide reduces the friction to visiting.

## Track to site visit, not to lead

The single change that transforms a real estate account: define your conversion event as a booked site visit, not a form submission.

If you feed the platform form fills, it optimises for people who fill forms. If you feed it booked visits — via offline conversion upload or a CRM integration — it starts finding people who show up.

That requires your sales team to log outcomes consistently. It is an operational discipline problem more than a marketing one, and it is worth solving.

## A realistic structure to start

1. One project, one campaign, three audiences.
2. Price stated, three qualification questions in the form.
3. WhatsApp automation on submission.
4. Follow-up sequence over fourteen days.
5. Site visits logged and fed back as conversions.
6. Review at day 30 on cost per site visit, not cost per lead.

Do that and the category stops looking broken.
